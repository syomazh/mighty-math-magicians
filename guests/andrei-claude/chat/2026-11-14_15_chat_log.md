**User (18:39):** Fuss-Catalans also count the number of lattice paths (with S and E steps) connecting (0,(q-1)n) and (n,0) and staying under the line connecting the points (right?); or the number of all S-E paths from (0,(q-1)n+1) to (n,0) up to a cyclic shift. Find a combinators/symmetry proof in terms of such paths

**Claude:** Both counts are right, and there is a symmetry proof on the paths. It is shorter than the tree proof because it needs no cherry collapse.

I checked both models by brute force for nine small (q, n). The paths weakly under the line number C = C_n^(q). The paths from (0, m) to (n, 0) form C classes under cyclic shift, each with exactly N = qn + 1 paths, because gcd(n, N) = 1.

*Setup.* Let W be all S–E paths from (0, m) to (n, 0), before quotienting. Then |W| = N·C and N ≡ 1 (mod p). So it is enough to show p^t divides |W| and |W|/p^t ≡ (−1)^t/∏ m_j! (mod p).

Write N_i, n_i, m_i for the base-p digits of N, n, m. Cut every path at the same places into N_i consecutive segments of p^i steps: one single step, and n_i segments of q·p^i steps. Each segment of p^h steps splits into p sub-segments of p^(h−1) steps, down to single steps. A **move** rotates the p sub-segments of any one segment cyclically. Two paths are equivalent if moves connect them.

*Proof.*

1. **Class sizes.** Call a segment symmetric if its p sub-pieces are equivalent, asymmetric otherwise. Let d(w) be the number of asymmetric segments. The class of w has p^d(w) paths, by the same induction as on slide 5.
2. **Lower bound.** For a piece with a E-steps and b S-steps, d ≥ (sₚ(a) + sₚ(b) − 1)/(p − 1). This is the induction of slide 6 (monochromatic piece: both sides are 0; symmetric piece: d = p·d(u₁) ≥ d(u₁), and the bound for (a/p, b/p) equals the bound for (a, b); asymmetric piece: d = 1 + Σ d(uᵢ), and digit sums are subadditive). Summing over the sₚ(N) top segments gives d(w) ≥ (sₚ(n) + sₚ(m) − sₚ(N))/(p − 1). This equals t, because sₚ(qn + 1) = sₚ(n) + 1. So p^t divides N·C, hence C.
3. **Minimal paths.** Equality forces every mixed (non-monochromatic) segment to be asymmetric, and no carries anywhere. So the maximal monochromatic segments ("atoms") are exactly n_i E-atoms and m_i S-atoms of length p^i.
4. **Mixed segments are the carries.** Let x_i be the number of mixed segments of length p^i in a minimal path. Counting segments of that length gives N_i + p·x_(i+1) = n_i + m_i + x_i, with x₀ = 0. That is the carry recursion for n + m = N. So x_i = c_i ∈ {0, 1}: one mixed segment per carry, t in total.
5. **Count.** Build a minimal path from the top level down. Level i has N_i + p·c_(i+1) slots: the top segments, plus the p children of the mixed segment above, if any. Fill them with n_i E-atoms, m_i S-atoms and c_i mixed segments. Every filling is minimal because n_i, m_i < p. Each class has p^t paths, so #minimal classes = ∏ᵢ (N_i + p·c_(i+1))! / (p^c_(i+1) · n_i! · m_i!). By Wilson, (N_i + p)!/p ≡ −N_i! (mod p). So the count is ≡ (−1)^t ∏ N_i!/(n_i! m_i!). The digits of qn + 1 are a 1 and the digits of n, so ∏ N_i! = ∏ n_i!, leaving (−1)^t/∏ m_j!.

*Example: p = 3, q = 3, n = 3.* Here N = 10, cut as 9 + 1, with one carry. The only minimal class is EEE·SSS·SSS·S and its two rotations of the 9-block. The other 117 paths lie in one class of size 9 and four of size 27. So 120 = 3·1 + 9·13, C = 12 = 3·4, and 4 ≡ 1 ≡ −1/(2!·1!) (mod 3).

*What I checked.* Brute force confirms steps 1–5 and the final congruence in two sets of cases: all binomials with N ≤ 16 for p = 2, 3, 5 (456 cases); 34 Fuss–Catalan cases up to N = 28, including q = 4, 8, 9. Class sizes were also confirmed by computing orbits directly. In your project's grades this is "computed" plus "ai-proved"; no second model has reviewed it and nobody has signed it.

*Caveats.*

- **The group acts on the N·C paths, not on the C classes.** Moves do not commute with cyclic shift and do not keep a path under the line. Passing to C uses the one arithmetic fact N ≡ 1 (mod p).
- **It proves more than asked.** Steps 1–5 work for any binomial coefficient, so this is a group-action proof of Kummer's theorem and of a classical congruence, specialised to C(qn+1, n). Granville's survey credits that congruence to Anton (1869), Stickelberger (1890) and Hensel (1902). The trees being rotated are the p-ary trees of segments, not Konvalinka's q-ary trees, so judges may not count it as "tree symmetries". That is your call.
- **Novelty is unverified.** The standard group-action proof of Lucas' theorem uses only a product of cyclic groups rotating blocks of p^i elements. Two quick searches did not turn up this nested-rotation version for Kummer and Anton, but I would expect it to exist.
- **Relation to the tree proof.** S-atoms play the role of the complete subtrees, and mixed segments play the role of the t asymmetric skeleton nodes. On paths the skeleton is rigid, so the count is a product. On trees the skeleton shape varies, which is why cherry collapse was needed.

**User (19:53):** (quoting the assistant's "Novelty is unverified…" paragraph) The main theorem (Theorem 17) is stronger than Lucas Theorem. Did it appear in literature?

**Claude:** Yes: as a statement, Theorem 17 is classical and about 150 years old. Only the proof by symmetry might be new, and I could not settle that.

*The statement.*

- **Exponent.** That p^c divides the binomial coefficient exactly, with c the number of carries, is Kummer's theorem from 1852.
- **Residue.** The congruence (−1)^e·C(n,m)/p^e ≡ ∏ n_i!/(m_i!·r_i!) (mod p), with r = n − m, goes back to H. Anton in 1869. That is Theorem 17 exactly. Granville's survey adds Stickelberger (1890) and Hensel (1902) as independent discoverers, "and many others since".
- **Relation to Lucas.** When there are no carries, m_i = N_i − n_i and the formula becomes Lucas' product of digit binomials. When there is a carry, Lucas only says "≡ 0", while Theorem 17 gives the exact power and the first nonzero residue.

So in the talk the safe wording is: "this is the classical Kummer–Anton congruence; what we add is a proof by symmetry."

1. Give the objects placed at that level distinct labels. They go into N_i row slots and p slots on a cycle, the cycle taken up to rotation. That gives (N_i + p)!/p classes.

**User (20:41):** I see, so Wilson is a trade off for cherry collapse in the distinct labels cover. Is there a proof of Wilson using a 'cherry collapse' argument? Does it appear in the literature?

**Claude:** Yes on both counts: the cherry collapse with a single cherry is a classical proof of Wilson's theorem, due to Julius Petersen in 1872.

*The proof, in your language.* Take k = 1 and a = (p): p labels of one class.
- The only tree shape is one cherry, so T(a) is the set of cyclic orders of p labels, and |T(a)| = (p − 1)!.
- Let σ cycle the labels. A cyclic order is fixed exactly when relabelling equals a rotation. That gives the p − 1 orders x₁, x₁₊ᵣ, x₁₊₂ᵣ, … for r = 1, …, p − 1.
- The collapse lands in T((1)), which has one element. So (p − 1)! ≡ (p − 1)·1 ≡ −1 (mod p).

So Wilson's theorem is the first case of your theorem |T(a)| ≡ (−1)^r, with r = 1. The general cherry collapse is this argument run inside a tree, with the other leaves as spectators.

*In the literature.*
- Gessel's survey talk on combinatorial proofs of congruences credits a proof of this kind for Wilson's theorem to Petersen. In that version, conjugation by a rotation permutes the (p − 1)! cyclic permutations of p points and leaves exactly p − 1 of them in place. That is the same count: "relabel by σ" is conjugation, and the fixed ones are the powers of the rotation.
- An online number theory text presents the same proof in Carmichael's words, with p-gons drawn on p equally spaced points of a circle. The fixed ones there are the regular polygon and the star polygons.
- Sagan's 1985 paper on congruences via group actions names Petersen's paper as the origin of the whole method.

So the single collapse step is not new, and a judge who knows this proof will recognise it. What you add is running it inside trees with classes, where the carries decide which collapses are possible. Slide 11's speaker notes already mention Petersen.

**User (22:26):** can the combinatorial/symmetry argument pushed to prove congruence results for the coefficients Fuss-Catalan polynomial (enumerations with respect to a statistic) or even for q-binomial coefficients?

**Claude:** Yes for statistics that the rotations preserve, and I tested one tonight. For q-binomials the divisibility half has a classical symmetric proof that lines up with your carries; the exactness and residue half looks open to me. I write x for the polynomial variable, since q = p^k is taken.

*Statistics on trees.*

**The principle.** If a statistic is constant on rotation classes, the enumerator is a sum over classes of p^d·x^stat. So every coefficient is divisible by p^t, and the enumerator divided by p^t is congruent mod p to the enumerator of the minimal classes.

**What qualifies.** Rotations preserve depth, so anything read from depths works: leaves per level, height, total path length. Statistics that depend on the order of children (leftmost leaves, area, major index) do not qualify.

**The residue.** It comes from a cherry collapse with exact depths instead of depths mod k. The carry is a → a − p·e_(δ+1) + e_δ with no wrap-around, and the invariant is the Kraft sum: Σ a_δ·p^(−δ) = 1.

**What I checked.** I counted q-ary trees by the number of leaves at each depth, for 29 triples (p, k, n) with q ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9}. Every count is divisible by p^t, and the residue matches the formula from the exact-depth collapse in all 29. The proof of that collapse is only a sketch.

**Example: q = 3, n = 6.** There are 1428 trees and 13 depth profiles, and all 13 counts are divisible by 3. Only one profile has a count not divisible by 9: one leaf at depth 1, three at depth 2, nine at depth 3, with 60 = 3·20 trees. In most cases I tested, a single profile carries the whole residue.

**Literature.** The nearest work in the team's references is on weighted Catalan numbers: Postnikov–Sagan, Konvalinka's Theorem 5, and Gao–Gu. Their weights count non-right edges, which rotations do not preserve. Their results need divisibility conditions on the weights and give congruence to the unweighted number. I have not checked whether the depth refinement appears there.

*x-binomials.*

**Carries are cyclotomic factors.** The cyclotomic polynomial Φ_{p^i}(x) divides the x-binomial [N choose n]_x exactly once for each carry into digit i, and Φ_{p^i}(1) = p. I verified this in 567 cases. Fray (1967) and Knuth–Wilf (1989) are among the references for Kummer-type results on q-binomials.

**Match with the path proof.** A mixed segment of length p^i corresponds to a factor Φ_{p^i}(x). For q = 9, n = 2 the x-Fuss–Catalan polynomial [N choose n]_x/[N]_x is Φ₃·Φ₉·Q(x), and the minimal path has mixed segments of lengths 3 and 9.

**Divisibility by symmetry.** The x-analogue of Lucas' theorem has had a group-action proof since Sagan's 1992 paper, with the idea credited to Strehl. In your setting it would go like this (my sketch, not checked against Sagan's paper): weight a path by x to the sum of the positions of its E-steps; rotate a segment of length p^i by one step; the weight is multiplied by x^a, where a is the number of E-steps in the segment; orbits cancel mod Φ_{p^i} unless the segment is all E or all S. The resulting congruence makes Φ_{p^i} divide the x-binomial exactly when there is a carry into digit i. That is an x-analogue of "p^t divides C" by one level of rotations.

**What is open.** I don't see a symmetric proof that each factor appears only once, or an x-analogue of the residue. The weight is not constant on the classes of the nested moves, and I don't know what the right x-statement for the residue would be.
