## Slide 1 — Title (~15 s, Syoma)
"Hello, we are the Mighty Math Magicians, and this is Problem 137: the power of a prime
dividing the Fuss–Catalan numbers, proved from symmetries of trees." State upfront that
the proof is a candidate under verification, and that the last slide explains exactly how
AI was used. "Our starting point is a slogan: a counting fact deserves a counting proof."

## Slide 2 — A pattern in the Catalan numbers (~1 min, Syoma)
Walk down the table slowly; the audience should discover the pattern with you.
"$C_6 = 132$ is divisible by $4$, and $7 = n+1$ has three $1$-bits; $C_7 = 429$ is odd,
and $8$ has one $1$-bit." Emphasize the corollary everyone can take home: $C_n$ is odd
exactly when $n+1$ is a power of $2$. "This is the fact we want to *see*, not compute."

## Slide 3 — The problem (~1 min, Syoma)
Define $q$-ary trees with the picture in your head: each node has $q$ ordered slots.
Give the Fuss–Catalan formula and the leaf count $m = (q-1)n+1$. State Konvalinka's
valuation formula as the known result, obtained through factorial valuations. "Problem 137
asks to replace that arithmetic argument by the symmetries of the trees themselves."

## Slide 4 — Konvalinka's symmetries and ours (~2 min, GS)
This is the conceptual pivot of the talk; slow down. Left: at each node Konvalinka allows
every permutation of the $q$ branches, so orbit sizes are products of multinomials, and
after three pages he gets divisibility — but exactness he still quotes from factorials.
Right: we keep only cyclic rotations, on a $p$-ary refinement; the group is a $p$-group,
so every orbit has $p$-power size, and the smallest orbits can be listed and counted
modulo $p$. "One change of group turns a divisibility argument into an exact count."

## Slide 5 — Step 0: a $q$-ary tree is a $p$-ary tree (~1 min, GS)
Explain the picture: each $q$-ary node opens into a complete $p$-ary block of height $k$,
whose $p^k = q$ bottom slots receive the original branches in order. Leaves keep their
number, and all leaf depths become multiples of $k$; this is a bijection. "From now on
all trees are $p$-ary, and $q$ survives only as the depth condition."

## Slide 6 — Step 1: rotate, don't permute (~1.5 min, GS)
Show the $\mathbb{Z}_3$ example: rotating $(A,B,A)$ gives three distinct trees because the
word is not constant; a constant word would give one. Define $\sim$ as the equivalence
generated by rotating at any node, and note that rotations never move a leaf to a different
depth, so the class condition survives. Finish with the key definition: symmetric versus
asymmetric nodes, and $d(T)$, the number of asymmetric nodes.

## Slide 7 — Class size $= p^{d(T)}$ (~1.5 min, GS)
Use the two colours: a blue symmetric node contributes no new rotations, a red asymmetric
one multiplies the class by $p$. The induction is one sentence: the $p$ rotations of a
word of prime length $p$ are either all equal or pairwise distinct. Then sum over classes:
$C_n^{(q)} = \sum p^d$, so the valuation is at least $\min d$, and modulo $p$ the quotient
sees only the minimal classes. "Everything now reduces to: what is $\min d$, and how many
classes attain it?"

## Slide 8 — Step 2: every class has at least $p^t$ trees (~1.5 min, GS)
State the lower bound $d(T) \ge t$ with $t = (s_p(m)-1)/(p-1)$. The only ingredients are
$s_p(pa) = s_p(a)$ and subadditivity of digit sums, with equality exactly when there are
no carries — no factorials anywhere. Sketch the induction: symmetric root multiplies $m$
by $p$ and $d$ by $p$; asymmetric root adds one to $d$ and merges digit sums. "This alone
reproves Konvalinka's divisibility in five lines."

## Slide 9 — Step 3: minimal trees are built from digits (~2 min, GS)
"Minimal" means $d(T) = t$, the smallest possible classes. Trace equality: every internal
node must be asymmetric, and between them hang complete trees — atoms. Carry-freeness at
every node means the digits of $m$ are just distributed among the subtrees, so there are
exactly $m_j$ atoms of height $j$: the atoms *are* the base-$p$ digits of $m$. State the
bijection with cyclic skeleton trees, including the depth condition modulo $k$, and note
that the skeleton has exactly $t$ nodes.

## Slide 10 — Running example: $p=2$, $n=6$ (~2 min, Syoma)
This is the slide to do with the audience. $C_6 = 132 = 4\cdot 33$, $m = 7 = 111_2$, so the
atoms have $1$, $2$ and $4$ leaves. The three pictures are all the ways to build a binary
skeleton on these atoms: choose which atom sits at the root. Each class has $2^2 = 4$ trees,
three classes account for $12$ of the $132$ trees, and $N_{\min} = 3$ is odd — which is why
the valuation is exactly $2$. Mention that these are rooted phylogenetic trees on three
species, the three $1$-bits of $7$.

## Slide 11 — Step 4: a carry is a collapsing cherry (~2.5 min, GS)
The hardest slide; keep it concrete. Cycle $p$ labels of the same class with a
$\mathbb{Z}_p$ action: modulo $p$ only fixed trees remain, and a fixed tree must carry the
$p$ moved labels as $p$ siblings — a cherry. Collapsing the cherry to one leaf of class
$c+1$ is exactly carrying $p$ units in base $p$, and the $p-1$ compatible cyclic orders
contribute a factor $-1$ each time. Iterate until one leaf remains: $t$ carries, so
$T(a) \equiv (-1)^t$. Point at the column addition: combinatorics on the left, arithmetic
on the right, same operation.

## Slide 12 — Conclusion (~1 min, GS)
Assemble: class sizes are $p^d$ with $d \ge t$, and modulo $p$ only the minimal classes
survive the sum. Their count is $(-1)^t / \prod m_j!$, which is prime to $p$, so the
valuation is exactly $t$ — and we get the residue of the unit part as a bonus. Stress that
no factorial, binomial or multinomial valuation was used. For $p = q = 2$ the minimal
classes are counted exactly by $(2s-3)!!$, an odd number.

## Slide 13 — Prime-power example: $p=2$, $q=4$, $n=4$ (~1.5 min, Syoma)
Show that the depth condition is not decoration. $C_4^{(4)} = 140 = 4\cdot 35$, $m = 13$,
atoms of sizes $1, 4, 8$. The atom $8$ must sit at odd depth, the other two at even depth,
so only one of the three possible skeletons survives: $N_{\min} = 1$, and indeed $140/4 = 35$
is odd. Read off the carries $(2,1)\to(0,2)\to(1,0)$: two carries, sign $+1$.

## Slide 14 — What else falls out (~1.5 min, Syoma)
Be explicit that everything on this slide is conjectural and under numerical test.
Weighted numbers: Konvalinka's weighted proof appears to need only transitivity, which our
group has. Level profiles: rotations preserve the number of nodes at each depth, so the
divisibility should refine to every profile. And the same argument works for any
rotation-closed family. "This is why we think the method, not just the answer, is new."

## Slide 15 — The idea in one sentence (~30 s, Syoma or GS)
Deliver the three lines slowly, as the takeaway: shrink the group to a $p$-group; the
smallest orbits are built from the digits of $m$; a carry is a collapsing cherry.
"Exactness — not just divisibility — comes free." This is the slide a judge should be able
to repeat back.

## Slides added by the orchestrator (2026-11-13 23:50)

**Slide 3, "The same trees, elsewhere" (Syoma).** Before stating the problem, show that these classes of trees are familiar. For ternary trees, rotating the three branches at a node is what a chemist does with the three other bonds of a carbon atom, and the number of classes is the number of alkyl radicals counted with stereoisomers. For binary trees the classes are the shapes of evolutionary trees. Our question is how many ordered trees sit over each shape; the answer is always a power of p.

**Slide 16, "Two more ways to see it" (grad student; optional, skip if short of time).** Two ideas that are not signed. Frobenius: modulo p the equation G = x + G^p forces G = x + x^p + x^(p^2) + ..., which already says when the count is prime to p. Accounting: with labelled leaves every node is worth a factor -p, the m! labellings absorb some of them, and t are left over.
