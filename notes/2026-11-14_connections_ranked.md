## 0. Verdict

Ranked by the criteria of the brief, after the independent review (section 7), which changed the order of the draft:

1. **Evolutionary trees (biology, p = 2).** The lower bound, the minimal trees and their odd number are theorems of a 2021 phylogenetics paper under the name "symmetry nodes"; "cherry" is that field's word. This is the candidate that holds most of the method, and it is prior work: known for p = 2, three times over, never for an odd prime.
2. **Sylow subgroups of the symmetric group (group theory, every p and q).** A tree is minimal exactly when its symmetries form a Sylow p-subgroup of the permutations of its leaves; the digits of m are the factors of that subgroup. The proof's skeleton, and the place where Legendre's formula would have entered. Mathematics, not another field.
3. **Alkyl groups (chemistry, p = 3).** Our classes are the stereoisomers, our ordered trees their staggered conformations, and the stabiliser is the symmetry number of the group, which a standard reference computes by collapsing symmetric rotors. It is the only place outside mathematics where the cyclic group of an odd prime is a physical rotation. The field has Step 1 and no more: no bound, no digits, no power of 3.
4. **Mobiles (every prime p).** The plan of the proof in one picture: a mobile with d lopsided hubs has p^d photographs. A model, not a field.
5. **Quadtrees (q = 4).** The only picture of a prime power: the eight symmetries of a square are exactly the group the proof puts at a 4-ary node, and the 22 pictures with three cuts fall into families of 2, 4 and 16.

Just outside: **stacked triangulations** (they tie with 4 and 5 and are the twin of 3 in geometry), **Feynman diagrams** (fourth in the draft; taken out after the review, section 4.1), **binomial queues** (the carry budget as an exact running cost), **sand on a ring** (the depth condition). Stockmayer's factor, rhythm trees, p-adic balls and the other "same numbers" items are at the bottom, as asked.

**For the slides: a recommendation, which is a judgment and not the ranking.** If one slide: the chemistry (3). It is new, it is an odd prime, which is where our theorem goes beyond the literature, and the picture of the five butyl groups carries the idea. If a second: the evolutionary trees (1), on the prior-work slide, as evidence that the lemma is natural. Sylow (2) is one sentence for the mathematicians in the room, and the quadtree (5) is the answer to "what about prime powers?".

Three findings matter beyond the ranking.

- Konvalinka's paper says in so many words that his group-action argument is a combinatorial proof of only a part of the formula: the divisibility. The exactness is the part that was missing, and it is our Step 4.
- For p = 2 the whole statement, including the number of minimal classes, is in Deutsch and Sagan (2006, Lemma 2.3), is restated by Gao and Gu (2021), and was found again independently by Kersting and Fischer (2021).
- Konvalinka's Lemmas 8 to 10 already describe the minimal orbits of the full symmetry group as a skeleton with complete trees counted by the base-q digits. Misha's slide 15 lists that shape among what is new; it needs a credit (section 5).

## 1. How the candidates were scored

In the order of importance given in the brief.

- **S, the step it contains (0 to 4).** 4: the power of p itself, or the exactness, is a fact of the other field (for the two candidates that have it: the facts that make the power exact are theorems there, though neither field draws our conclusion about tree numbers). 3: the rotation group and the class sizes (Step 1) are a fact of the field, or it is a faithful model of them. 2: one step appears as an operation or as a statement, without the group (the carry count, the atoms from the digits, the depth condition). 1: a loose analogy to a step, or the same proof principle in another setting. 0: only the numbers are shared.
- **E, exactness of the statement (0 to 2).** 2: an exact statement, checked by computer here or a theorem in a source. 1: an exact core with an analogy around it. 0: analogy only.
- **F, the field recognises it (0 to 2).** 2: it is in the field's own vocabulary and a source of that field states it. 1: the field would recognise the objects, but the statement is ours. 0: no practitioners.
- **P, one picture in 30 seconds (0 to 2).**
- **N, novelty (a label, not a score).** K: known in the literature. K2: known for p = 2 only. O: the link to our theorem is apparently ours (searched, nothing found; a search cannot prove absence).

Candidates are ordered by S, then E, then F, then P; candidates with equal scores are left in the order of the draft, and rows 4, 5 and 6 are such a tie. Steps are numbered as in the paper: Step 0 (a q-ary node is k levels of p-ary nodes), Step 1 (rotation classes of size p^d), Step 2 (d ≥ t, the carry budget), Step 3 (minimal trees from the digits of m; the depth condition for q = p^k), Step 4 (the cherry collapse; exactness).

## 3. The top five

### 3.1 Symmetry nodes and cherries of evolutionary trees (p = 2)

**Exact statements.** The first two are the field's own theorems; the third is ours, in the field's vocabulary.
1. A rooted binary tree shape with n leaves and s symmetry nodes (nodes whose two subtrees have the same shape) has n!/2^s labellings by n species. Steel derives it by orbit and stabiliser: the labellings of a shape are n!/|Stab|, the stabiliser has order 2^s, and it is in general built from wreath products (Steel 2014, section 2 [50]; also Bóna and Flajolet [41]; Ford, Lemma 31 [42]; the group is A(T_1) × A(T_2) when the two subtrees differ and a wreath product with Z_2 when they are equal, a theorem of Jordan as quoted by Billey, Konvalinka and Matsen [51]). Checked: E015 K3a.
2. The **symmetry nodes index** of a tree is its number of non-symmetry nodes, (n − 1) − s: this is our d. Its minimum over trees with n leaves is wt(n) − 1, where wt is the number of ones in binary, attained exactly by the trees obtained from a "top tree" with wt(n) leaves by hanging fully balanced trees of sizes 2^i for the binary digits 1 of n; and there are (2·wt(n) − 3)!! such trees, an odd number (Kersting and Fischer 2021, Theorems 3.5 and 3.9 [4]; OEIS A344852). The index is implemented in two R packages, the authors' `symmeTree` and `treebalance`.
3. Two leaves with a common parent are a **cherry** (the field's word: Wu and Choi, section 2 [51]). Exchanging two species in a labelled tree gives the same tree exactly when they form a cherry; there are (2n − 3)!! labelled trees ([50], pp. 4–5), an odd number, and (2n − 5)!! of them are unchanged. The statement about the exchange is ours; checked: E015 K3a (n ≤ 7). For p = 3 and 5 the analogue is: a cyclic permutation of p labels fixes a labelled tree only if those labels form a cherry, and it fixes p − 1 of the (p − 1)! cyclic arrangements of that cherry (all of them for p = 3, 4 of 24 for p = 5): E015 K3b.

**Which step, and how.** Statement 1 is Step 1 in its labelled form (orbit and stabiliser under the permutations of the species). Statement 2 is Steps 2 and 3 for p = 2, k = 1, and its odd number (2·wt − 3)!! is the conclusion that makes the power of 2 exact: the number of minimal classes is not divisible by 2. **The field does not use the argument of our Step 4 and does not need it:** for p = 2 the atoms have different sizes, so the minimal trees are counted directly by the known number of labelled trees. Statement 3 shows what Step 4 looks like in the field's vocabulary; "cherry" is a shared word, not a shared argument. Nor does the field have the conclusion about Catalan numbers: Kersting and Fischer never mention them.

**Known or ours.** Known for p = 2, three times: Deutsch and Sagan 2006, Lemma 2.3 [2] has the bound and the odd count of minimal orbits, by the automorphism group of the complete binary tree; Gao and Gu [60] restate it as a theorem on the structure of minimal orbits and say that the q-ary case is Konvalinka's; Kersting and Fischer have the same bound, the same trees and the same count, and their reference list does not contain Deutsch and Sagan. The two fields do not cite each other. Ours: odd primes, prime powers, the residue. The word "cherry" for the Step 4 configuration was chosen in our paper independently of this. Vocabulary: Kersting and Fischer say "symmetry nodes", Steel "symmetry vertices", Etherington "balanced knots" [36]; our "symmetric node" is none of these exactly, and for a talk "symmetry node" is the word to use.

**Figure (draft).** `talk/figures/fig_phylo_cherry`: the 15 trees on four species; the 3 with the cherry {a, b} stand still when a and b are exchanged, the other 12 pair off.

**Two sentences for the talk.** "Biologists measure how lopsided an evolutionary tree is by counting its symmetry nodes, and in 2021 they proved that a tree with n species has at least as many lopsided nodes as n has binary ones, minus one: that is our Step 2 for p = 2, found without knowing it was about Catalan numbers. Our last step is in their vocabulary too: swapping two species changes nothing exactly when they form what they call a cherry."

**Strongest objection.** "So for p = 2 your theorem was already known, and in two literatures." **Answer.** Yes, and the talk should say so on the prior-work slide (Misha's slide 15 already names Kersting–Fischer and Deutsch–Sagan). For p = 2 a rotation and a reflection are the same thing, which is why the binary case was within reach of both. The connection is offered as evidence that the lemma is natural, and that the notion that generalises is "symmetric node under cyclic rotation", not as a new result. A biologist has no use for the cyclic group at a node with three branches.

### 3.2 Sylow subgroups of the symmetric group (every p, every q = p^k)

**Exact statements.**
1. The group our proof puts at a q-ary node, rotations at k levels of p-ary nodes, is the k-fold wreath product of cyclic groups of order p. It has order p^((q−1)/(p−1)) and is a Sylow p-subgroup of the group of all q! permutations of the branches (the order: Kaloujnine 1948, p. 241 [43], who describes the group by tables of polynomials and not by wreath products; the wreath-product form: Giannelli and Law, section 2, and Erdmann and Tan [44]; for p = 2, Deutsch and Sagan, Lemma 2.2 [2]). Checked for q = 4: E012 B (8 moves against 24).
2. The symmetries of a tree with n nodes, m leaves and d asymmetric nodes (the rotations that give back the same tree) form a group of order p^(n−d) that permutes the leaves faithfully. Checked: E015 K1b (explicit permutation groups, 17 classes).
3. That group is a Sylow p-subgroup of S_m exactly when the tree is minimal: n − d ≤ ν(m) = exponent of p in m!, with equality exactly when d = t. Checked: E015 K1a (p = 2, n ≤ 9; p = 3, n ≤ 6; p = 5, n ≤ 4); E011 C.
4. A Sylow p-subgroup of S_m is a direct product, over the base-p digits m_j of m, of m_j copies of the Sylow p-subgroup of S_(p^j) (Kaloujnine 1948, p. 239 [43]), and that one is the j-fold wreath product (Giannelli and Law, section 2, for odd p, as a standard fact [44]). The factors are our atoms. Its order p^ν(m), ν(m) = (m − s_p(m))/(p − 1), follows from the two statements of Kaloujnine; it was not found written in this form in an opened source. The construction in exactly our form, nested blocks taken from the base-p digits and cyclic rotations of the sub-blocks of each block, with the statement that these generate a Sylow p-subgroup, is in J. H. Smith (1993), Section 1 [62] (read after the first version of this note). The number of Sylow p-subgroups of S_m is checked by brute force for small cases (E015 K1d).

**Which step, and how.** Step 0 is statement 1: Konvalinka lets all q branches be permuted; we keep a Sylow subgroup, so that every orbit has p-power size. Step 1 is orbit and stabiliser: class size p^d = p^n/p^(n−d). Step 2 in this language is one line: the symmetry group is a p-subgroup of S_m, so its order divides m!, so n − d ≤ ν(m). **That line is Legendre's formula, the route the problem excludes.** Our Step 2 proves the same inequality from carries, with no factorial (it is machine-checked: `PTree.lower_bound`). Step 3 is statement 4. Step 4 is the standard tool of Sylow theory: when a group of order p acts on a finite set, the number of points is congruent modulo p to the number of fixed points (the "fixed-point congruence" of K. Conrad's notes on the Sylow theorems, equation (2.1); the same device as McKay's proof of 1959, in which a cyclic group of order p shifts the p-tuples of group elements with product 1 [54]).

A companion fact from the theory of groups acting on rooted trees: the "adding machine" is the element that rotates the p branches at the root and then continues inside one branch only; it acts on the leaves as adding 1 in base p, the continuation being the carry (Demir and Saltan [45]).

A second companion: Wielandt's proof of Sylow's theorem (1959) has the shape of our theorem. As described in a report on its formalisation and on Wikipedia [54] (Wielandt's paper was not opened), it lets the group act on the subsets of size p^α and uses the exact power of p in a binomial coefficient to find an orbit whose stabiliser is a Sylow subgroup. Ours finds the exact power of p in a tree number from the orbits whose stabiliser is a Sylow subgroup.

**Known or ours.** The structure of Sylow subgroups of symmetric groups is classical. Deutsch–Sagan and Konvalinka use automorphism groups of complete trees with the full symmetric group at each node. The statement "a tree is minimal exactly when its symmetry group is Sylow in S_m" is in our carry-budget note (claim C19); it was not found in the literature (two searches by the reviewing agent as well). It is a restatement of d ≥ t once the orders are known, not an independent fact. Of Step 4 only the general congruence is Sylow theory; the count of fixed points is ours.

**Figure (draft).** `talk/figures/fig_sylow`: m = 7 = 4 + 2 + 1; the three atoms with 2^3·2^1·2^0 = 16 symmetries; 7! = 16 × 315; a minimal tree with 7 leaves carrying them.

**Two sentences for the talk.** "The symmetries of a tree with m leaves form a group of p-power order inside the permutations of the leaves, and the trees that decide the answer are exactly those where this group is as large as such a group can be: a Sylow subgroup. Sylow subgroups of symmetric groups are built from the base-p digits of m, which is why the digits appear."

**Strongest objection.** "Then your Step 2 is Legendre's formula in disguise, and the problem asked you to avoid it." **Answer.** In group language the bound does follow from Legendre in one line, and that is precisely the proof we do not give. Ours derives the bound from the subadditivity of digit sums, and the Lean file that checks it contains no factorial. The dictionary is an explanation of why the digits of m appear; it also shows that the tree proof re-derives the exponent of p in m! as a by-product, which the accounting note makes explicit. A second, fair objection: this is mathematics, not another field. It is listed because it contains the skeleton of the method for every p and every q, and because Misha's slide 3 already uses the word.

### 3.3 Alkyl groups: stereoisomers, conformations and symmetry numbers (p = 3)

**Exact statements.**
1. **Classes are stereoisomers.** The rotation classes of ternary trees with n internal nodes are the alkyl groups C_nH_(2n+1)– with stereoisomers counted separately: 1, 1, 2, 5, 11, 28, 74 for n = 1 to 7 (OEIS A000625 [38]; the entry's word "steric" means that the three children of a node are taken in a cyclic order, a rotation giving the same tree and any other permutation a different one). Checked: E015 K2, E012 A.
2. **Ordered trees are conformations.** The number of ordered ternary trees, binom(3n, n)/(2n + 1), is described in the OEIS, from the chemical literature on staggered conformers, as the number of positions in space of the conformers of an alkyl group relative to another group without threefold symmetry; divided by the rotation of the whole group about its attachment bond it is the number of staggered conformers of alkyls, 1, 1, 4, 19, 91, 476 for n = 1 to 6 (OEIS A121179 [52], a comment of 2019 and five papers of Cyvin and co-authors, 1995 to 1998; the papers themselves were not opened). Checked: E015 K10 (ordered ternary trees up to rotation at the root give A121179, n ≤ 8). So a class with d asymmetric nodes is one stereoisomer in 3^d conformations: each carbon whose three branches are not all alike has three distinguishable staggered positions (the chemists' trans, gauche-plus and gauche-minus [53]), and each carbon with three like branches has one; this is said of a group on a partner without symmetry of its own, for example in an alcohol (on a single atom such as chlorine the turn at the root is a rotation of the whole molecule and shows nothing). (Such a carbon is a stereogenic centre or a prochirality centre; an earlier version said "chirality centre", which is narrower: pseudo-asymmetric centres are stereogenic and not chirality centres; corrected 2026-11-15 after the review in `notes/2026-11-15_review_rotors_and_guest_notes.md`. It need not be what chemists call an asymmetric carbon, which has four different groups: the CH2 of an ethyl group is an asymmetric node of ours and not an asymmetric carbon of theirs. Correction proposed by the guest andrei-claudecode; with chemists in the room say "lopsided" or "not a threefold rotor".) In the same vocabulary, the hydrogens that our rotations interchange are the chemists' homotopic ones; the two hydrogens of a CH2 are exchanged only by a reflection, are called enantiotopic (or diastereotopic when the molecule is chiral), and are two different orbits in the model (worker W029, from open textbooks; the guest, from the IUPAC glossary). For the most symmetric groups the orbits on the hydrogens have sizes 3^j with multiplicities the base-3 digits of the number of hydrogens (computed by the guest up to 8 carbons and by W029 up to 5); the converse fails, first at 7 carbons. **Limits found by the review of 2026-11-15** (same note): that binom(3n, n)/(2n + 1) counts conformations in the chemical literature rests on the OEIS comment only, so a slide should present the 55 as our count of positions and not say that chemists count 55 conformations; of the symmetry numbers 81, 9, 9, 9, 3 of the butyl picture only 81 is printed (for the tert-butyl radical with rotating methyl groups and for 2-methylpropane), and no opened source gives a symmetry number for an alcohol; "homotopic" is meant in the sense of the substitution test and of internal rotation, not of a rotation axis of the molecule.
3. **The stabiliser is the symmetry number of the group.** For an alkyl group attached to something without symmetry of its own, 3^(n−d) is its **internal symmetry number** by the rule thermochemists use: a methyl group counts 3; a symmetric terminal rotor is "spun" into a pseudo-atom and the process is repeated; a non-symmetric rotor contributes nothing (Domalski and Hearing 1988, Appendix D [39]; read in the source by the orchestrator). Their worked examples of a group on a benzene ring agree directly: toluene 3 × 2 (methyl, ring), tert-butylbenzene 27 × 3 × 2 (tert-butyl 81, ring). **Their values for whole alkanes are not values of the model.** An alkane is two groups joined at a bond, and the paper's total is the product of the two groups' numbers times the symmetry that moves that bond, which a rooted tree does not see: ethane 18 = (3 · 3) · 2, 2-methylpropane 81 = (81 · 1) · 1, 2-methylbutane 27 = (9 · 3) · 1, 2,2-dimethylbutane 243 = (81 · 3) · 1, 2,2-dimethylpropane 972 = (81 · 3) · 4, 2,2,3,3-tetramethylbutane 13122 = (81 · 81) · 2, methane 12 = 3 · 4. Checked: E015 K8 (3 groups, 8 alkanes; the last factor is listed, not predicted). The first version of that check carried the value 3 for ethane as if it were the paper's; it is not (the paper has 9), and an independent agent caught it.
4. So for every alkyl group, **(number of conformations) × (symmetry number of the group) = 3^n**. For the five butyl groups: 1 × 81, 9 × 9, 9 × 9, 9 × 9, 27 × 3. Checked: E015 K8b. Of these five symmetry numbers only 81 appears in the source as a worked example; 9 and 3 follow from its rule and are in no table.
5. The theorem, in these words: among alkyl groups with n carbons the largest internal symmetry number is 3^ν with ν = (m − s_3(m))/2 and m = 2n + 1 the number of hydrogens (Step 2); it is attained exactly by the groups assembled from hydrogen, methyl, tert-butyl, tri-tert-butylmethyl (1, 3, 9, 27 hydrogens) according to the base-3 digits of m (Step 3); and the number of conformations, binom(3n, n)/(2n + 1), is divisible by 3 to the power t = n − ν exactly. For n = 4: 55 = 1 + 9 + 9 + 9 + 27 is prime to 3 because tert-butyl has the full symmetry number 81 = 3^4. Checked: E015 K2 (n ≤ 7).

**Which step, and how.** Step 1: the rotation at a node is the rotation of a group by 120° about the bond that joins it to the rest of the molecule. It is cyclic, and a reflection would turn the group into its mirror image, which is a different stereoisomer: this is why the proof uses rotations and not all permutations. The class size is 3^n divided by the symmetry number: orbit and stabiliser. The field also has Step 1 at the root as a formula: the number of conformers in A121179 is the ternary tree number divided by 3, with a correction exactly when n ≡ 1 (mod 3), that is, for the groups with threefold symmetry about the attachment bond; and the functional equation in A000625, A(x) = 1 + x(A(x)³ + 2A(x³))/3 (the entry cites Pólya 1936, equation (25)), is the same count for classes. This is the divisibility by 3 in its first instance, and the p = 3 form of the equation our Frobenius note starts from. **That is as far as the field goes.** It has no bound on the symmetry number (Step 2), no digits (Step 3), no count modulo 3 (Step 4) and no power of 3 beyond the first. Two things are only resemblances: our atoms are perfectly branched groups, of which methyl and tert-butyl are everyday chemistry; and Domalski and Hearing's procedure (replace a symmetric rotor by one pseudo-atom, repeat) is the same move as our cherry collapse, but it serves to compute the stabiliser, which is Step 1, not to count fixed points. The exactness has a chemical reading only when t = 0: the count is prime to 3 exactly when a perfectly branched group exists (n = 1, 4, 13, 40). These are the n of OEIS A201823 [70], which lists the ternary tree numbers not divisible by 3 and states that they are ≡ 1 (mod 3): the case t = 0 of our theorem for p = 3, with its residue, is in the OEIS, without proof.

**What the physics adds.** The symmetry number enters the entropy as −R ln σ, so each symmetric node costs R ln 3 ≈ 9.13 J/(mol·K) ([39], section 3; [11]). Spectroscopists describe the same internal rotations by wreath products of cyclic groups [40], which are the groups of our Step 1.

**Sources.** [38] OEIS A000625 (it cites Blair and Henze 1932, Pólya 1936 and Robinson, Harary and Balaban 1976 [71–73]; opened in part by the guest andrei-claudecode from the scans on the OEIS server, and their counts agree with our enumeration up to 14 carbons. Robinson, Harary and Balaban, p. 358, give the group at a carbon as the cyclic group of order 3 because the corner of the tetrahedron that points to the root stays fixed: a printed source for "rotated, not reflected"); [52] OEIS A121179; [39] Domalski and Hearing; [53] Flory's Nobel lecture for the three rotational states of a bond; [40] Balasubramanian; [11] Gilson and Irikura. Reference [12] does not support the entropy statement and is no longer cited for it. **Known or ours:** the stereoisomer count, the conformer count and the symmetry-number rule are standard in chemistry. The statement "symmetry number = 3^(symmetric nodes)" is Domalski and Hearing's procedure in our words; the complementary statement "3^(asymmetric nodes) conformations" was not found stated. The dictionary to our theorem, and the reading of the theorem as a bound on symmetry numbers, are apparently ours: nothing was found that links A000625, A121179 or symmetry numbers to the power of 3 in the ternary tree numbers (searches listed in the agents' reports; A000625 has no cross-reference to A001764).

**Figure (draft).** `talk/figures/fig_symmetry_number`: the five butyl groups with both numbers under each, and the line 1·81 = 9·9 = 27·3 = 3^4. Its row "symmetry number" is the internal symmetry number of the group on a generic partner; say so when showing it.

**Two sentences for the talk.** "Each of the four carbons of a butyl group can be turned into three positions, 81 settings: for tert-butyl all 81 look alike (81 is the symmetry number of the group, the kind of number chemists put into the entropy), while the straight chain has 27 different shapes. Our classes are these molecules, our ordered trees are their shapes, and 55 = 1 + 9 + 9 + 9 + 27 is not divisible by 3 because tert-butyl has only one shape."

**Strongest objection.** "This is the counting of isomers by Blair and Henze and by Pólya, known since the 1930s. And your numbers are not in the tables: the reference you cite gives 18 for butane, 81 for isobutane and 9 for ethane, where your trees give 3 for the n-butyl group, 9 for isobutyl and 3 for ethyl." **Answer.** Both true. The tables are for whole molecules; the model is a group attached to something without symmetry, R–X, which is the convention of the conformer literature too. The numbers for groups come from the source's rule and agree with its two examples of a group on a ring; a whole alkane is two groups, and its tabulated number is the product of theirs times a factor 1, 2 or 4 that the model does not contain (statement 3). So the slide must say "symmetry number of the group", not "the symmetry number in the tables", and the product rule holds only for independent rotors. A real molecule also does not realise every staggered conformation (for five carbons and more, some would put two atoms in the same place); the formula in the OEIS entry counts them all, as we do, and what the chemical papers do about it was not checked, since they were not opened. What is ours is not the count of isomers but the statement that the power of 3 in the ternary tree numbers is decided by the most symmetric groups, and it holds in the model exactly; the chemistry supplies the meaning of the group and of the two numbers, not a proof. It is also only p = 3.

### 3.4 Mobiles (every prime p)

**Exact statements.**
1. Trees whose branches at each node are taken up to cyclic rotation are called mobiles in combinatorics (OEIS A032200 [46]); for p-ary trees their numbers are the columns of A398107 [25], which for p = 2, 3, 5 are A001190, A000625, A036727.
2. For a prime p, a mobile with d lopsided hubs has exactly p^d photographs (ordered trees); spinning a balanced hub changes nothing. Checked: E013 `c8_mobiles_necklaces.py`; E015 K0. For a prime power this fails: a hub with four arms turns in 4 ways, the proof's group at such a node has 8 elements, and the families of 4-ary mobiles do not have sizes 4^d (the reviewing agent's computation for 3 to 6 nodes). The picture for q = 4 is 3.5, not a mobile.
3. So the Fuss–Catalan number is a sum of powers of p, one for each mobile, and its power of p is the smallest that occurs, provided the number of mobiles with that smallest power is not divisible by p. For p = 2 and 4 leaves: 5 = 1 + 4.

**Which step, and how.** Step 1 for every prime, with the reason for rotations: a hub with three or more arms can be turned but not mirrored. Statement 3 is the plan of the whole proof. Steps 2 and 3 are "the mobiles with the fewest photographs".

**Known or ours.** The object and its name are known. The reading of the theorem is ours. **This is a model, not a field:** nobody studies hanging mobiles, and no source is claimed for the sculpture. It contains Step 1 and nothing of Steps 2 to 4. One caution about the word: according to the reviewing agent (from search results only, not verified) "mobile" is now more often used for a different kind of labelled tree in the theory of planar maps; say "trees up to turning the branches at each node" once.

**Figure.** `talk/figures/fig_mobile` exists (one mobile, four photographs). Draft added: `talk/figures/fig_mobile_plan`, the two mobiles with four leaves and their 1 + 4 = 5 photographs.

**Two sentences for the talk.** "Hang a tree from the ceiling: every bar can spin, so one mobile has many photographs, 2 for each lopsided bar. The number of photographs of all mobiles is the Catalan number, and the power of 2 in it is set by the mobiles that have the fewest."

**Strongest objection.** "This is your own definition with a nice name; and a real mobile seen from the other side is mirrored." **Answer.** Right on the first: it is a picture of Step 1, not evidence from another field, and it should be presented as such. On the second: seen from behind, every hub is mirrored at once, which is one global reflection and not an independent reflection at each hub; for p = 2 it makes no difference.

### 3.5 Quadtrees: cutting a square (q = 4)

**Exact statements.**
1. A quadtree is a square cut into four quarters, some of which are cut again, and so on; it is a standard way to store images and maps (Samet's survey [24]; bibliographic record only, the text was not opened). The pictures with n cuts are the 4-ary trees with n nodes, binom(4n, n)/(3n + 1) of them: 1, 4, 22, 140 for n = 1 to 4 (OEIS A002293 [25]).
2. The eight symmetries of a square permute its four quarters and keep the two diagonals together. As permutations of the quarters they are exactly the group our proof puts at a 4-ary node, which reads the node as two levels of binary nodes, ((NW, SE), (NE, SW)), and rotates at each: exchange the two diagonals, or exchange the two quarters of one diagonal. It has order 8, a power of 2, although there are 24 permutations of four things. Checked: E015 K11 (the two groups are equal as sets of permutations); E012 B.
3. So turn or mirror any cut square in place. The pictures fall into families of 2^d members, d being the number of asymmetric binary nodes. With three cuts, 22 = 2 + 4 + 16. The smallest family has 2 = 2^t pictures with t = 1, because the picture has m = 10 cells and 10 = 1010 in binary; and there is one such family, an odd number. The number of families, 1, 1, 3, 6, 17 for 1 to 5 cuts, is OEIS A398109 [25] (4-ary mobiles with the dihedral group at each node). Checked: E015 K11 (n ≤ 5); E013 `c12_subdivisions.py`, by a geometric model with no trees in it.

**Which step, and how.** Step 0, and this is the only picture of it: for a prime power the group at a node is not all q! permutations (Konvalinka's choice, with families of sizes like 6 and 12) and not the q rotations of a hub (a mobile, 3.4), but the group in between, here the symmetries of the square. Step 1 as statement 3. The depth condition of Step 3 is visible in the smallest family: its two pictures have one diagonal pair of quarters cut and the other not, so the atoms are "a diagonal pair, both cut" (8 cells) and "a diagonal pair, uncut" (2 cells). An atom of 2 cells is half a square, and it has to sit at half a level: that is the condition that an atom of size p^j sits at a depth ≡ −j modulo k.

**Known or ours.** Quadtrees are standard; the count of families under the dihedral group is in the OEIS since July 2026. The identification of the proof's group with the symmetries of the square, and the reading of the theorem as a statement about families of pictures, are ours.

**Figure.** `talk/figures/fig_quadtree` exists: the 22 pictures in their three families.

**Two sentences for the talk.** "Cut a square into four quarters and cut some of the quarters again: with three cuts there are 22 pictures. Turn or mirror any cut square in place and they fall into families of 2, 4 and 16, so 22 is divisible by 2 and not by 4; and the eight symmetries of the square are exactly the group our proof uses when q is 4."

**Strongest objection.** "That the symmetries of a square are a Sylow subgroup of the permutations of four things is an accident of q = 4. For a cube cut into eight it fails, so this is not a picture of prime powers." **Answer.** Right: for q = 8 the proof's group has 128 elements and a cube has 48 symmetries, and for q = 9 a square cut into nine has 8 symmetries where the proof needs 81. It is a picture of the smallest prime power, and what it shows is the one idea of Step 0: the right group is neither everything nor a single rotation. Nobody in computer graphics sorts quadtrees into these families; the field supplies the object, not the statement.

## 4. Strong candidates that did not make the top five

### 4.1 Worked out, then taken out of the top five: symmetry factors of Feynman diagrams (p = 2)

This was fourth in the draft. The independent review (section 7) found three things, each of which I checked and accept; after them it is the same mathematics as 3.1 in another notation, and it stays as a remark for an audience of physicists.

**What is exact.**
1. Take a field with a cubic interaction and a source J, and the field it produces at one point, at tree level. Its diagrams with m sources are the rooted binary trees with m leaves up to exchanging branches, the root being the point of observation and the leaves the sources: our classes for p = 2. A diagram is divided by its symmetry factor, the number of automorphisms that keep the external point fixed, and that number is 2^(number of symmetric vertices) = 2^(n−d). Checked against a direct count over labelled trees for m ≤ 8 and by recursion for m ≤ 20 (E013 `c3_feynman.py`); the reviewing agent recomputed it by direct enumeration of graph automorphisms for m ≤ 12.
2. With the coupling written g/3!, the sum of 1/(symmetry factor) over the diagrams with m sources is C_(m−1)/2^(m−1). For m = 7: 3/16 + 3/8 + 4/4 + 1/2 = 33/16, and three diagrams, an odd number, have the largest factor 16 = 2^ν(7).

**What did not hold up.**
- *"The form 2^(symmetric vertices) is ours."* It is not. That the automorphism group of a binary tree shape has order 2^(number of symmetry nodes) is in Bóna and Flajolet [41] (section 4, as read by the reviewing agent), and their count of labelled trees is statement 2 in another form. Only the reading in terms of the power of 2 is ours.
- *"The denominator of the amplitude is the largest symmetry factor."* The denominator is there only because the coupling is written g/3!, which puts a factor 1/2 at every vertex. Write the coupling as g/3 and the coefficients are the Catalan numbers themselves. What does not depend on the convention is (number of ordered trees in a class) × (symmetry factor) = 2^(m−1), and that is orbit and stabiliser, statement 1 of 3.1.
- *"Tree amplitude."* When the external lines are distinguished, as for a scattering amplitude, tree diagrams have symmetry factor 1 (Srednicki, chapter 10 [55]). The factors appear only for identical sources and one marked point. Without the marked point they are not even powers of 2: three sources on one vertex can be exchanged in 6 ways.

**Sources.** [26] for the setting (their equation is the Schwinger–Dyson equation with another sign convention; φ = J + (g/2)φ² is our reading of it). [55]: Etingof's lecture notes, Theorem 3.5, weight each graph by 1/|Aut|, the automorphisms fixing the external vertices; Srednicki's textbook draft, chapter 9, works out S = 2·2·2 = 8 for the cubic tree diagram with four sources and two vertices; Frabetti (sections 6.3 to 6.5, opened by the reviewing agent) writes the classical field with a cubic interaction as a series over trees, each divided by the number of permutations of the sources that fix it. No source was found that links symmetry factors to the power of 2.

**Figure (draft).** `talk/figures/fig_feynman`: the eleven diagrams with 7 sources in rows by symmetry factor, and the sum 33/16. Its last line speaks of "the denominator"; if the figure is used, add "with the coupling written g/3!".

**If it is used at all,** one sentence: "A physicist would say it this way: every tree diagram is divided by its symmetry factor, and the diagrams with the largest symmetry factor, of which there is an odd number, are the ones that decide the power of 2."

### 4.2 The others

- **Binomial queues, #9.** The best meaning for the carry budget. Vuillemin (1978, pp. 310–312) [9]: a queue of n items has one tree for each binary 1 of n; merging two queues costs exactly ones(n) + ones(n′) − ones(n + n′) comparisons; building from n singletons costs n − ones(n), which he identifies with the number of carries in 1 + 1 + … + 1. That the merge cost is Kummer's count of carries is our remark (he does not name Kummer). Checked: E015 K5b, K9. Okasaki's "numerical representations" [47] states the principle (inserting is incrementing, linking two trees of equal rank is the carry). No symmetry is involved, which is why it is not in the top five.
- **Merging in any base, #11.** Bentley and Saxe (1980) [56]: a growing collection is kept as one static structure for each binary 1 of its size, and insertion is binary counting; on p. 323 the same with zero, one or two structures of each power of 3, matching the ternary digit, and then in radix k. This is "p equal pieces merge into one of the next size" with the digits named, in the source that the storage literature descends from. (The papers on log-structured merge trees that were opened describe the merging but not the digits.) Checked: E015 K5.
- **Sand on a ring, #13; Kraft's equality modulo q − 1, #15; the Internet checksum, #27.** Three faces of the depth condition for q = p^k: carries that wrap around a ring of k positions. The first is exact and already on Misha's slide 14; the second is an exact remark; the third is an analogy from a standard: RFC 1071 [48] adds 16-bit words with "end around carry", and the note appended to it (IEN 45, 1978) explains this as arithmetic with check factor 2^16 − 1. Checked for our digits: E015 K7.
- **Stacked triangulations, #6.** The geometric twin of 3.3 with the same numbers (12 = 3 + 9, 55 = 1 + 9 + 9 + 9 + 27). The bijection with ternary trees is Proposition 1 of Albenque and Marckert [58], who call it classical. It ties with mobiles and quadtrees on every score and is left out of the top five only because it repeats 3.3 in another material; the OEIS entry behind 3.3, statement 2, also links a paper on stack polytopes [52].
- **Knockout tournaments, #14.** The quickest way to say Step 2 to anyone: 100 entrants, binary 1100100, at least two matches between differently shaped halves of the draw. That knockout formats are counted by the Wedderburn–Etherington numbers is in the literature (Oshima; OEIS A096351 has n!/2^(balanced matches) for the number of draws [57]); the statement about unbalanced matches is ours.
- **McEliece's theorem, #33.** Not the method, but a theorem of the same shape in coding theory: the weights of a cyclic code are all divisible by a power of p given by a minimum of digit sums, and one weight is not divisible by the next power; its proof is Stickelberger's congruence [49]. Worth mentioning to the professor together with claim C21.
