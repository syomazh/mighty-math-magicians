## 1. The best five (new since the first round), ranked

Ranking criteria per the brief: step contained (divisibility or exactness first), exactness, recognisability with a real source, one-picture-in-30-seconds, novelty.

1. **Touchard's congruence for the Bell numbers (set partitions), every prime p.** The standard proof contains our Step 4 word for word: the cyclic group C_p acts on the set partitions of {1,…,p+n} by cyclically permuting 1,…,p; the count B_{p+n} is congruent mod p to the number of fixed partitions; the fixed partitions are exactly of two kinds — 1,…,p in one common block ("collapse the fixed block into a single point", giving B_{n+1}) or all singletons (giving B_n) — so B_{n+p} ≡ B_n + B_{n+1} (mod p). This is an exact, named, classical congruence decided modulo p by its most symmetric objects, with the same carry/collapse geometry as our cherry-to-atom step. Sources: statement read in full on Wikipedia "Bell number"; proof read in full in Gessel's slides (see §3); original works: J. Touchard, "Nombres exponentiels et nombres de Bernoulli", Canad. J. Math. 8 (1956), DOI 10.4153/cjm-1956-034-1, and "Sur les cycles des substitutions", Acta Math. 72 (1939), DOI 10.1007/bf02547349 (OpenAlex records and abstract read).
2. **Petersen's 1872 wheel proofs of Fermat's and Wilson's theorems.** The origin of the whole fixed-point-of-a-p-cycle principle: a wheel with p spokes colored in a colors gives a^p ≡ a (mod p), because rotation classes have size 1 (the a monochromatic ones) or p; and the (p−1)! cyclic permutations of p points under conjugation by C_p give (p−1)! ≡ −1 (mod p) (p−1 fixed cycles). Both are exact statements mod p read off the fixed configurations — our Step 4's engine with no trees at all, in the oldest source of the argument. Sources: Gessel, "Combinatorial Proofs of Congruences", JMM 2010 slides, full text read (PDF fetched, pdftotext, kept then deleted per brief); Wikipedia "Proofs of Fermat's little theorem", full article read (necklace section says it is "an adaptation of Golomb's proof").
3. **Golomb's 1956 necklace proof (the 30-second picture).** S. W. Golomb, "Combinatorial Proof of Fermat's 'Little' Theorem", Amer. Math. Monthly 63 (Dec. 1956), DOI 10.2307/2309563 (Crossref record read; open PDF located via search results). The p-bead strings over an a-letter alphabet fall into friend-classes of size p except the a constant strings; for p = 5, a = 2 the 32 strings are listed explicitly on Wikipedia (full article read) — the best one-picture illustration of "every non-fixed orbit has size p", i.e. of our Step 1 + Step 4 combined.
4. **OEIS A201823: the exactness of Fuss–Catalan divisibility for q = 3 already sits in a database.** Record read via the OEIS API: the terms of A001764 (ternary trees, the Fuss–Catalan numbers for q = 3) not divisible by 3 are exactly a(n) = A001764((3^n−1)/2) = binom(3(3^n−1)/2, (3^n−1)/2)/3^n, and a(n) ≡ 1 (mod 3). This is our theorem's conclusion for p = 3, k = 1: at m = 3^n the digit sum is 1, t = 0, and the residue is +1 — matching the residue (−1)^t/Π m_j! = 1. So a link from Fuss–Catalan divisibility to a published sequence (with the exact residue) exists; adjacent records found: Lin, "The odd Catalan numbers modulo 2^k" (arXiv:1012.1756), Eremin, "Factoring Catalan numbers" (arXiv:1908.03752), Gao–Gu, "Arithmetic of weighted Catalan numbers" (J. Number Theory 2021) — all OpenAlex records only.
5. **Kummer's theorem (1852): carries with an exact p-adic meaning, for binomial coefficients.** Wikipedia "Kummer's theorem", full article read: v_p(binom(n,m)) equals the number of carries when adding m and n−m in base p (Kummer 1852, "Über die Ergänzungssätze zu den allgemeinen Reciprocitätsgesetzen", J. reine angew. Math. 44, DOI 10.1515/cjfm… as recorded), with the digit-sum form v_p = (s_p(m)+s_p(n−m)−s_p(n))/(p−1). This is the carry mechanism of our Step 2 wearing an exact p-adic valuation in the literature — but proved via Legendre's formula (the route our problem excludes), which is why it stays at rank 5 despite being exact.

## 3. Answer to E (the two names on the slide) — found

- **"Petersen" = Julius Peter Christian Petersen (1839–1910), Danish mathematician.** Per Ira M. Gessel's slides "Combinatorial Proofs of Congruences" (JMM, January 2010; full text read via fetched PDF + pdftotext): "In 1872 Julius Petersen published a proof of Fermat's theorem a^p ≡ a (mod p): we take a wheel with p spokes and color each spoke in one of a colors; two colorings are equivalent if one can be rotated into the other. An equivalence class has size 1 iff all spokes have the same color (a of them); every other class has size p, since orbits under a cyclic group of order p have size 1 or p. Hence a^p ≡ a (mod p)." The same slides: "Petersen also gave a similar proof of Wilson's theorem (p−1)!+1 ≡ 0 (mod p): there are (p−1)! cyclic permutations of p points; the cyclic group C_p acts on them by conjugation, viewed geometrically as rotation; there are p−1 fixed cycles, so (p−1)! ≡ p−1 (mod p)." So Petersen 1872 is the origin of the fixed-point-of-a-p-cycle counting principle itself, applied to colorings of a p-wheel (Fermat) and to p-cycles in S_p (Wilson). The exact 1872 paper title was not located (Crossref/OpenAlex searches surfaced only unrelated 1872 works by other Petersens) — see §4 "leads not verified".
- **"Golomb" = Solomon W. Golomb, "Combinatorial Proof of Fermat's 'Little' Theorem", American Mathematical Monthly 63 (Dec. 1956), DOI 10.2307/2309563** (Crossref record read). The necklace/beads form of the same p-cycle argument; Wikipedia's "Proofs of Fermat's little theorem" (full article read) presents it as "an adaptation of Golomb's proof".
- **"Touchard" = Jacques Touchard (1885–1968).** The congruence named after him (Wikipedia "Bell number", full article read): for prime p, **B_{n+p} ≡ B_n + B_{n+1} (mod p)** (and B_{pm+n} ≡ m·B_n + B_{n+1}); the Bell numbers are periodic mod p. Original works (OpenAlex records read): J. Touchard, "Nombres exponentiels et nombres de Bernoulli", Canad. J. Math. 8 (1956), DOI 10.4153/cjm-1956-034-1 (abstract read; the "nombres exponentiels" are the Bell numbers), and "Sur les cycles des substitutions", Acta Math. 72 (1939), DOI 10.1007/bf02547349. **The standard proof (Gessel's slides, full text read) is literally our Step 4**: C_p acts on set partitions of {1,…,p+n} by cyclically permuting 1,…,p; B_{p+n} ≡ (number of fixed partitions) (mod p); the fixed partitions are exactly (1) 1,…,p all in one block — collapsed into a single point, giving B_{n+1}; (2) 1,…,p all singletons, giving B_n. Hence B_{n+p} ≡ B_n + B_{n+1} (mod p). "Count the fixed points of a p-cycle, collapse the fixed block" = the p special points merging into one block and being replaced by one point — the same carry/collapse as our Step 4's cherry-to-atom.

Relevance to the talk: the slide's "Petersen/Touchard" idea is the fixed-point congruence for a cyclic group of prime order, with fixed configurations obtained by collapsing a p-block into one point. It occurs classically in colorings of a p-wheel (Fermat, Petersen 1872), p-cycles under conjugation (Wilson, Petersen 1872), beads on a string (Golomb 1956), and set partitions (Touchard's congruence). None involve trees; our Step 4 is the same engine on labelled atoms sitting at tree nodes, and our residue (−1)^t/Π m_j! is the analogue of Touchard's B_n + B_{n+1}.
